The outcome: three values, five principles

Version dated July 8, 2026 — the framework this repository tracks feedback on. Three shared values, grounded in UNESCO's Open Science recommendation and the Dutch NPOS2030 ambition document; five principles, each broken into subprinciples and the actions that put them into practice. See how it was formed below.

How these values and principles were formed

Sixty people picked apart Open Science infrastructure for an afternoon at SURF's Utrecht office on July 1, 2026. A week later, the arguments had settled into the table above.

8breakout-group posters
72challenges named

A four-year run-up

The Dutch open-science community agreed on shared ambitions in 2022, when the National Programme Open Science (NPOS) published its NPOS2030 Ambition Document with five guiding principles. Three became this framework's values, quoted almost verbatim: quality and integrity, collective benefit, and equity, fairness, diversity and inclusion. The other two, reworded, became its first two principles — "as open as possible, but as restricted as necessary" became Openness, and "academic and digital sovereignty must be safeguarded" became Autonomy.

Turning ambition into infrastructure took longer. Open Science NL, the funding body set up in 2023, began financing open-infrastructure projects. Sixteen organizations — among them OCW, NWO, Universities of the Netherlands (UNL), NFU and SURF — formed a standing national consultation to coordinate. By November 2024 they had agreed to develop a joint project: the Strategic Plan for Integrated Infrastructure for Open Science, or SPII. A first phase delivered results by mid-2025; a second phase, SPII 2026, started that April with a mandate to build a reference model for the whole landscape. The July 1 consultation was its opening move.

The day itself

John Doove of SURF chaired the afternoon; Darco Jansen of Universities of the Netherlands introduced the programme. Maurits van der Graaf presented a proposed reference model — guiding principles, a landscape overview, shared terminology and an interoperability framework — before the room split into breakout groups.

Fifty-nine attendees answered a live poll on which infrastructure they represented, with more than one answer allowed. Research data management projects made up the largest group (13, plus 2 more in research software management), followed by disciplinary infrastructures (12), publication and CRIS systems (12 combined), open-science coordination and policy roles (11), infrastructure-as-a-whole roles (6), open research information (3) and hardware (2).

Organizers walked in with a skeleton already sketched: the three NPOS-derived values, Openness and Autonomy as principles, and Sustainability, Interoperability and Researcher-centric proposed alongside them. The breakout groups' task wasn't to invent from a blank page — it was to add or change the subprinciples, and to name the practices that would put each principle into effect. Seven people per table, one poster each.

What the posters said

The eight groups worked from a shared template — principles down the left, subprinciples and consequences across — and filled it in with sticky notes.

"Processes are key. Describe the process of the research journey (interacting with the systems in and outside your uni)."

— breakout poster 1, Openness column

"Risk assessments: using a commercial system might cost more than developing in-house — but you can stop paying, not sack staff."

— breakout poster 1, Sustainability column

"Make sure you have USERS!"

— breakout poster 1, Researcher-centric column

The closing plenary polled the room again on the day's broader challenges: 72 answers, which organizers sorted into themes. Governance topped the list at 22 mentions, ahead of fragmented infrastructure connections (13), sustainability (11), interoperability (6), user needs (6), security (3), closed-source software (2) and shared terminology (2).

Not everyone was convinced

The closing feedback wasn't uniformly positive. Among the written remarks organizers recorded:

"We have discussed the principles for years already. Focus on use cases and implementation."

"Please convince me that we need additional principle harmonizing work given that the universities have already signed off the NPOS principles."

"Too abstract."

Other attendees asked for the consultation itself to widen: invite researchers directly, connect to related national projects, and "don't limit review & discussion of the reference model as a whole to the Open Science festival only." That comment turned out prescient — a dedicated review workshop was already scheduled for the Open Science Festival on Oct. 14. All of it is recorded verbatim in the raw Mentimeter export, linked below.

From sticky notes to a table

Organizers spent the following week turning eight posters and dozens of Mentimeter answers into a single draft: five principles, their subprinciples, and the practices that would put them into effect. Not every suggestion survived the cut — a table of ideas left out, from "protection layers for AI where needed" to "invest in engagement of board members," is preserved in full in the archive below.

The version published July 8, 2026 — the table at the top of this page — grounds the framework's three values directly in UNESCO's Open Science recommendation and the NPOS ambition document, and its principles in a wider set of existing frameworks: the TRUST Principles for digital repositories, the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure (POSI), the Seven Guiding Principles for Open Research Information — the same 2022 document deliverable 1B's Principles Alignment Tool now offers as its own 7GPRI framework — and a forthcoming national open-access strategy.

From SPII to POSI+

By late September 2026 the question had shifted from what the principles should say to how they would land. In an email to the principles subgroup, the SPII project's external consultant, from Pleiade Management and Consultancy, passed on a hesitation raised in a regular meeting with two people involved in Open Science NL's upcoming infrastructure call. Applicants to that call will be asked to look at POSI, the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure. A second, separate set of principles from SPII could confuse them.

POSI's momentum is international. In a conversation for another client, Crossref's director told the consultant that Crossref was working to embrace the latest version of POSI, and the list of POSI adopters on its website keeps growing. The risk, the consultant wrote, was that SPII principles standing on their own would meet little enthusiasm. The proposal:

"Present the SPII principles as complementary to the POSI principles."

— the SPII project's external consultant, translated from Dutch

In practice, the assessment tool would cover POSI first — an earlier version of it already did — and then the SPII principles as an addition, leaving out the ones that overlap. That, the consultant expected, would make the principles easier to include in the Open Science NL call and build wider support.

The subgroup picked it up within a day. The SPII project lead noted that it echoed an idea a subgroup member from Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam had raised before the summer, and, with NWO deadlines approaching, proposed meeting that same week. The subgroup member from SURF who maintains the Principles Alignment Tool called a POSI+ "a smart move" and set out the work: assess which parts of POSI overlap with SPII, add the SPII parts that are missing, and make that clear in deliverable 1A — this page — first. The Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam member agreed: adding a maturity scale to the POSI principles would make it "a very useful tool."

Three, four or five levels?

One question stayed open. POSI self-assessments often use a three-point scale. The draft SPII maturity matrix uses four levels, and its author has argued for four or five, so that there is always a difficult ambition left to reach for. The Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam member asked the practical counter-question: how many relevant levels can actually be described for each POSI principle?

To speed things up, the tool's maintainer offered to draft a first POSI+ with AI, on the explicit condition that the team reviews it carefully, in line with the EU AI Act's transparency rules. The POSI+ v0.5 proposal and its four-level maturity matrix below are that draft: AI-assisted, not yet reviewed, and meant to be taken apart. They also try to answer the scale question, by showing how POSI's three points fit inside a four-level scale.

What happens next

This table is a draft, not a finished standard: the subprinciples under Researcher-centric are thinner than the rest, and the actions list still reads more like a first pass than a settled taxonomy. It is also the direct basis for the SPII v0.1 framework in deliverable 1B's Principles Alignment Tool — so a correction made here should eventually be reflected there too. If the subgroup adopts the POSI+ direction, POSI+ becomes the version the tool offers first. Use the feedback link above to propose a change to either.

POSI+ v0.5: SPII as an extension of POSI

Draft proposal, September 29, 2026. It compares the SPII principles — the July 8, 2026 outcome above and the v0.3 maturity matrix below — with the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure (POSI v2.0, 2025), and proposes a merged set: all twenty POSI principles, unchanged, followed by only those SPII principles that POSI does not already cover. See "From SPII to POSI+" in the story above for why.

Drafted with AI (Claude) from the sources linked on this page, and not yet reviewed by the SPII team. Every judgement below — which items overlap, where each SPII idea lands, and the wording of the SPII additions — is a proposal for the principles subgroup to accept, change or reject.

The pattern is clear. POSI already covers most of what SPII says about governance, finance, transparency and enduring availability — two SPII actions (regular review, mission-consistent revenue) even use POSI's own wording. What POSI lacks is almost everything SPII says about interoperability beyond open standards, about researchers as users, and about digital sovereignty, compliance and operational resilience.

What POSI adds that SPII did not have

Seven POSI principles have no clear SPII counterpart. In POSI+ they come along unchanged — a net gain for SPII:

  • Coverage across the scholarly enterprise — no SPII counterpart; sits in tension with SPII's Discipline-specific
  • Cannot lobby — no SPII counterpart
  • Goal to generate surplus — no SPII counterpart beyond general financial sustainability
  • Establish and maintain financial reserves guided by policy — no SPII counterpart
  • Revenue generated from services, not data — no SPII counterpart
  • Volunteer labour — only indirectly, under Human capacity
  • Patent non-assertion — no SPII counterpart

The proposal: POSI+ v0.5

Thirty-seven principles in eight sections. The first three sections are POSI v2.0, quoted verbatim, so an infrastructure's existing POSI self-assessment stays valid as the first part of a POSI+ one. The last five hold the seventeen SPII additions, grouped under the SPII principle they come from. SPII's three values — quality and integrity; collective benefit; equity, fairness, diversity and inclusion — have no counterpart in POSI and stay as the shared commitments above all thirty-seven.

Considerations for the review

  • Different subjects. POSI is written for the organisation that runs an infrastructure: its board, its money, its insurance against failure. SPII also looks outward, at the infrastructure's users and its place in the ecosystem. That is why the SPII additions cluster under Interoperability and Researcher-centric.
  • Coverage versus discipline-specific. POSI asks infrastructures to serve the whole scholarly enterprise; SPII asks them to fit a discipline's specific needs. POSI+ keeps both. Curators should decide whether that is a contradiction or two sides of one good practice.
  • POSI verbatim, and the name. Keeping POSI's text unchanged is what makes POSI+ an extension rather than a competitor. Whether "POSI+" is an acceptable name is for the POSI Adopters to say — worth asking them before it is used publicly; "POSI with SPII extensions" is a fallback.
  • Three points inside four levels. A POSI self-assessment's three points can be read onto the four-level scale: not met ≈ Level 1, partly met ≈ Level 2, met ≈ Level 3. Level 4 goes beyond POSI compliance. Existing POSI self-assessments can then be carried over, while the ambition the four-level scale was meant to keep stays in place.
  • Can every POSI principle carry four levels? Mostly yes, but three are close to yes-or-no: Cannot lobby, Revenue generated from services not data, and Patent non-assertion. Their Levels 2 and 4 are thin. Curators can accept that, or ask for fewer levels on those principles — the 1B tool currently supports one scale per framework.
  • Reusing the v0.3 matrix. Level descriptions from the v0.3 matrix are kept verbatim wherever they exist, including its typos, and attached to the POSI+ principle they fit best. Some fits are loose. The v0.3 "Open source" levels are about using open source software, while POSI asks for all software needed to run the infrastructure to be released under an open-source licence — that requirement sits somewhere between v0.3's Levels 3 and 4. Similarly, v0.3's "Financially secure" levels are attached to POSI's "Time-limited funds", and "Human capacity available" to "Transition planning".
  • SPII ideas not carried as separate principles. Open to participation is folded into POSI's Non-discriminatory participation; Make-or-buy and Total cost of ownership into Digital sovereignty; Open licences and Terms of use into Legal interoperability. Say so if any of them deserves its own line.
  • Version number. v0.5 follows the SPII v0.1 framework (the July 8 outcome) and the v0.3 matrix.

POSI+ v0.5 maturity matrix

Four levels for each of the thirty-seven POSI+ principles, written with the spii-maturity-levels guidance: the same subject at every level, each level including the one before, observable statements in at most about 25 words, and no invented numeric thresholds. The shape of the scale follows the four inspiration frameworks named under the original maturity matrix; some wording reuses concepts from the other frameworks in the Principles Alignment Tool (GORC, FAIR, BD, OSR, 7GPRI), noted per principle in the CSV.

  • Level 1 — nothing done. No practice, no documentation, no owner, or the situation is unknown.
  • Level 2 — a start. Something exists, but partial, informal, or dependent on one or two people.
  • Level 3 — established. Systematic, documented, owned by a named role or body. Roughly where a POSI "met" lands.
  • Level 4 — the vision. Full, open, verified from outside, shaped with the community; others take it as the example. Deliberately ambitious.

Level texts marked "Verbatim from SPII maturity matrix v0.3" are unchanged from that matrix. Everything else was drafted with AI (Claude) for this proposal and is pending curator review; the "To improve" line under each principle names the most likely next step.
